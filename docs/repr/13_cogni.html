10. Lecture 13: Cognitive Science and Spatial Information

★☆☆ Follow Along:

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

So far, we have learned how to represent spatial information using GIS.

But there is another important question:

How do people understand spatial information?

When we look at a map, we do not simply see points, lines, and polygons.

We interpret them.

For example, when we look at a map of a city, we may think:

  • Where am I?

  • Where is my destination?

  • Which direction should I go?

  • How far is it?

  • Which route should I take?

These are questions studied in cognitive science.

Cognitive science studies how people perceive, remember, and understand information.

Spatial information is closely related to these processes.

One important concept is mental representation.

People can construct an internal representation of a place.

For example, after walking around a city several times, you may be able to remember:

Station
   |
   |
School ---- Park
   |
   |
Supermarket

You may not remember the exact coordinates.

Instead, you may remember the relationships between places.

This kind of internal representation is sometimes called a cognitive map.

A cognitive map is not necessarily an accurate map.

For example, people may remember two places as being closer together than they really are.

Therefore:

Geographic space
        ↓
Human perception
        ↓
Mental representation

The way people understand space can be different from the way GIS represents space.

★★☆ Follow Along:

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let's think about a simple navigation problem.

Suppose you are standing at a station.

You want to go to a supermarket.

A GIS can represent the locations using coordinates.

station = (139.7000, 35.7000)

supermarket = (139.7050, 35.7030)

The GIS can calculate distance and direction.

For example:

dx = supermarket[0] - station[0]

dy = supermarket[1] - station[1]

print(dx)
print(dy)

But humans do not normally think in longitude and latitude.

A person may instead think:

"Go north from the station,
 then turn right at the intersection."

This is a different representation of the same spatial information.

We can therefore distinguish between:

GIS representation
longitude / latitude
       ↓
spatial information
↓
human representation
landmarks / directions / routes

This difference is important when we design maps.

A map that is geographically accurate is not necessarily easy for people to understand.

For example, a navigation map may emphasize:

  • landmarks

  • roads

  • stations

  • intersections

  • relative direction

rather than showing every geographical detail.

This is one reason why map design and human cognition are closely connected.

★★★ Try Yourself:

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Think about a place that you know well.

For example:

  • your university

  • your hometown

  • a railway station

  • a shopping area

Try to draw the place without looking at a map.

Do not worry about geographic accuracy.

Draw the places and connections that you remember.

For example:

  Station
     |
     |
  Convenience Store
     |
  Intersection
    / \
   /   \
School  Cafe

Now compare your drawing with an actual GIS map.

Ask yourself:

  • Which places did you remember?

  • Which places did you forget?

  • Did you change the distances?

  • Did you change the direction?

  • Which landmarks were important?

The purpose of this exercise is to understand that spatial information is not only something that can be measured; it is also something that people perceive and remember.

In GIS, we represent space.

In cognitive science, we ask how people understand that space.
